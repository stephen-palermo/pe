It is my strongest pleasure to recommend Steve for the Principal Engineer (PE) role. I have collaborated with Steve for over a decade on the DCG Architecture and Power & Performance team, and in that time he has proven himself to be a true technical expert whose inventions directly shape the architecture and manufacturing of Intel's Data Center Group (DCG) products.

Steve is the inventor of Intel's workload-optimized CPU technologies, captured in his patent "Workload Optimized CPU" (US20230217253A1), which was granted just a few months ago as US12348969. This is not a paper invention. The architecture framework described in that patent is exactly the framework we productized: it established the technical foundation for Intel's workload-optimized SKU stack on the Sapphire Rapids architecture. Steve's core insight—and what makes the patent so impactful—is that silicon manufacturing and architecture should be changed and adapted based on real, measured benchmarks rather than theoretical targets. His framework takes actual workload telemetry and performance data and feeds it back into how cores, accelerators, and on-die resources are allocated and binned, so that each SKU is tuned to the workloads customers actually run. That closed loop between benchmark evidence and silicon definition is now part of how we build Xeon.

More recently, Steve has extended this same patented silicon framework into the Xeon Edge AI space. He created a series of architecturally directed, architecture-setting benchmarks that leverage the silicon technologies in his patent, all aimed squarely at improving Intel DCG products. He founded and flagged the Xeon Edge AI computer-vision architecture benchmark (the 8x8x8 benchmark) in direct partnership with my Power & Performance team, giving us a new, repeatable foundational benchmark for distributed edge solutions. He flags architecture-setting benchmarks across computer vision, small language models such as Google's Gemma, and agentic workloads within a distributed edge solution space—each one translating real customer AI requirements into measurable silicon trade-offs. Because these benchmarks are built on the manufacturing-and-architecture-adapts-to-benchmarks principle from his patent, they feed directly back into our P-core vs. E-core allocation, integrated Ethernet, and AI-accelerator decisions for upcoming Xeon silicon.

It is worth reinforcing exactly how the architecture framework described in that patent works, because it is the mechanism that makes his new benchmarks actionable at the silicon level. The patent ("Systems, methods, and apparatus for workload optimized central processing units") defines a workload analyzer that measures a running workload and derives an application ratio—a ratio Steve based on the operating frequency and core count required to execute that workload—and then uses that ratio to configure and adapt the CPU to the workload. This application-ratio analyzer is the loopback technology Steve used: real benchmark telemetry from a live workload is measured, reduced to the application ratio, and fed back as the control signal that tunes core allocation, frequency, and power/energy-efficiency targets of the silicon. It closes the loop between the workload and the part, which is precisely why it carries energy-efficiency (Y02D) and power-management claims that matter to my team. His new Xeon Edge AI benchmarks plug directly into this same loopback: the 8x8x8 computer-vision benchmark, the Gemma SLM benchmark, and the agentic edge benchmarks each generate the measured workload signatures that the application-ratio mechanism consumes, so the distributed edge workloads customers actually run become the inputs that drive our P-core/E-core, accelerator, and power trade-offs. In other words, the patent supplies the measure-ratio-reconfigure engine, and Steve's benchmarks supply the real workloads that engine optimizes against.
